## Overview

Designing a quadruped gait requires coordinating four feet while tracking a speed command and keeping the body upright. Instead of prescribing complete leg trajectories, Zashchitin and Dobriborsci specify the order of foot contacts and let a reinforcement learning policy learn the motions. The study asks how closely the learned gaits match their references and how much mechanical work they require in simulation.

## Methodology

The authors train policies with proximal policy optimisation (PPO) on an ANYmal model on level ground. The controller outputs torques for 12 joints. Its reward includes velocity tracking and penalties for joint effort, abrupt motion and foot slip. An optional reward for matching the reference contact sequence guides the policy towards a canter, half bound, pace, rotary gallop or trot, without fixing the duration of each stance or swing phase.

The study compares two training setups: one relies more on reward penalties, while the other adds stricter episode termination criteria. Each trained policy is evaluated in 10-second straight-line simulations at commanded forward speeds of 1, 2 and 3 m/s. Energy efficiency is summarised by the dimensionless cost of transport:

$$
\mathrm{CoT} = \frac{E}{mgd}
$$

Here $E$ is the mechanical work estimated from actuator effort and joint displacement, $m$ is the robot mass, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $d$ is distance travelled. Lower values indicate less mechanical energy per unit of weight and distance; they do not measure battery consumption.

## Results

The learned contact patterns generally matched their references, although the rotary gallop differed from its reference sequence (Fig. 2). The test tables show a trade-off: **10 of 18** policy–speed combinations completed after looser training, compared with **17 of 18** after stricter training (Tables 2–3). These counts include the policies trained without a gait reference. Completion means the simulation ran without violating the test limits, not that the gait carried a formal stability guarantee.

At a commanded 3 m/s, the policy trained without a reference gait completed the test under both setups, with a CoT of **1.18** after looser training and **1.46** after stricter training. Without a reference, the authors generally observed trot-like motion after looser training and canter-like motion after stricter training. Comparing CoT alongside completion matters: even runs that exceeded the test limits were assigned CoT values in the paper's tables.

## Conclusion

Foot-contact sequences sufficed to guide PPO toward distinct gaits in simulation. Stricter training constraints improved completion at a generally higher mechanical cost. The comparison does not establish a universal ranking of gaits or show that the learned policies will transfer to hardware.
