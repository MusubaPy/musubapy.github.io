## Overview

A gripper finger needs predictable fingertip motion for a precision pinch, but it needs extra mobility to wrap around an object whose shape is not known in advance. Borisov and colleagues propose a design procedure that gives one finger both modes with one drive motor. Fixing the length of an added link constrains the finger to a prescribed motion; allowing the link length to vary restores the mobility needed to conform on contact.

## Structural and Parametric Synthesis

The procedure starts with a planar finger and counts its degrees of freedom. If the base mechanism has $W_0$ degrees of freedom, the added linkage must contribute $W_f = 1 - W_0$ when its variable-length links are held fixed. The negative value denotes net constraints, not negative physical mobility. For a group of $n_f$ moving links and $P_f$ lower kinematic pairs, the authors use $W_f = 3n_f - 2P_f$ to enumerate candidate groups. They then identify where each group can attach and which of its links could change length.

For each candidate, the procedure first fixes the variable-length links at their minimum feasible lengths and tunes the geometry to follow a desired trajectory in the one-degree-of-freedom mode. It then bounds how far those links can extend while keeping the output motion inside the required workspace. The final steps call for force analysis in both modes and parameter optimization to reduce joint reaction forces and the required drive torque.

## Results

The paper works through the structural stage for two fingers. The two-phalange example starts with two degrees of freedom; the authors show how to add one movable link with two joints, or three movable links with five joints, to impose one net constraint. The three-phalange example starts with three degrees of freedom; the illustrated four-link, seven-joint group provides the two required constraints. In each case, the examples show how to obtain one degree of freedom with the variable-length links locked.

These are kinematic design examples. The paper lays out the subsequent optimization steps but does not report optimized finger dimensions or hardware grasp tests.

## Conclusion

The contribution is a way to design a change in mobility into the finger rather than reproduce both modes through control alone. The two examples show how to select and attach candidate linkages; whether a particular design delivers the desired workspace and grasp forces remains to be evaluated.
