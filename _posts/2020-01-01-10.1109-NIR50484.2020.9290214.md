## Overview

Energy efficiency is a fundamental constraint for legged robots operating on limited onboard power.
The leg mechanism architecture — specifically whether the kinematic chain is open or closed — has a significant but often underestimated effect on the energy budget.
Open kinematic chains use serially connected links, each joint driven independently; closed chains (parallel linkages such as pantograph mechanisms) route forces through a structural loop.
This paper analyses the two paradigms and quantifies the energetic consequences of this choice for locomotion tasks.

## Mechanism Analysis

In an open kinematic chain, actuators at each joint must overcome not only the payload inertia but also the inertia of all distal links. This concentrates mass at the end-effector side of the limb and requires the proximal joints to do disproportionate work. Closed-chain mechanisms relocate the actuators proximal to the body, transmitting motion through passive links. The result is a lighter, lower-inertia distal structure with reduced actuator torque requirements for a given leg trajectory.

The paper formalises this comparison through an energy model that accounts for both joint torques and velocities across a locomotion cycle. The analysis covers generic leg geometries and parametric regimes representative of practical legged robot designs, allowing the conclusions to generalise beyond a single hardware instance.

## Results

The comparison demonstrates that closed-chain leg mechanisms achieve lower energy consumption over equivalent locomotion trajectories when compared to open-chain counterparts of similar workspace. The structural advantage is most pronounced at higher stride frequencies, where the inertial contribution of the distal mass in open chains becomes dominant. The paper identifies the geometric and mass-distribution parameters that govern the crossover between regimes, providing design guidelines for selecting the appropriate architecture based on the target operating envelope.

## Conclusion

The choice between open and closed kinematic chains is not merely a structural preference — it is an energy design decision. This analysis provides a principled basis for that choice and motivates the adoption of closed-chain leg architectures in energy-constrained legged robots. The findings are directly applicable to the design of quadruped and hexapod systems where locomotion efficiency is a primary requirement.
